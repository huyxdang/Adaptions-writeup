Keith and Huy both work in finance, and we entered Adaption Labs’ AutoScientist Challenge with a practical goal: train a model that could actually be useful in our day-to-day work.

That goal came from a recurring problem we saw firsthand. Institutional finance reports contain the kind of analysis we wanted a model to learn—forecasts, valuations, recommendations, and the reasoning behind them—but they do not contain the prompts needed to turn that analysis into training data.

Our main challenge was therefore to reconstruct those prompts from the evidence available in each report without leaking the analyst’s final conclusion into the input. If done correctly, the model would need to arrive at the judgment itself rather than simply recover the answer from the source.

We faced many challenges. We repeatedly refined the data methodology, manually inspected examples, and checked that the resulting tasks still reflected real equity-research workflows. Along the way, we learned a great deal about both finance-data preparation and how to work effectively with AutoScientist.

Reading the numbers. Every score reported here is a pairwise preference: the share of comparisons in which a judge chose the adapted model’s answer over the base model’s. A score of 77 therefore does not mean 77% correct.

Attempt #1: Proof of Concept

Data

We started with roughly 1,500 sell-side reports on Vietnamese listed companies, published between 2011 and 2026. The collection included earnings reviews, valuation updates, and target-price revisions written by professional analysts. It did not include model-written text, blogs, or forum posts.

These reports contained the language we wanted the model to learn, but not the training structure we needed. An analyst writes a conclusion and the evidence behind it; the report does not record the instruction that produced that conclusion. Supervised fine-tuning requires both the instruction and the answer.

Extraction

Several early extraction approaches failed for the same reason: they generated plausible tasks that the source report did not actually answer. The version we kept works in reverse. It finds a real analyst conclusion first, removes that conclusion from the available evidence, and then reconstructs only the question that the evidence can support.

Earlier fixed templates asked every example for counterevidence and an invalidation condition, even when the source contained neither. In a 50-row review, 45 rows failed this check. We replaced the fixed task with one derived from each conclusion, then used quality gates to reduce the result to a smaller, traceable baseline.

Adaptive Data

We uploaded the 589 training rows to Adaption. Its Adaptive Data pipeline expanded them to 27,862 rows, of which 27,405 were used for training. The platform's quality score increased from 5.0 to 9.4, moving the dataset from grade C to grade A.

Adaptive Data changed the response structure more than the task. The prompt stayed close to the extracted evidence, while the completion became easier to inspect and evaluate.

AutoScientist

AutoScientist trained a LoRA adapter on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct. The run used rank 16, alpha 32, all linear layers, two epochs at 1e-4, 382 steps, and loss on completions only. It produced two preference evaluations.

The second result is the stronger one. We constructed the first evaluation distribution; Adaption constructed the broader category evaluation. Winning 77 to 23 on that category supports transfer beyond our own rows. It is a preference result, not an accuracy score.

Limitations

Attempt 1 established a useful signal, but it did not establish a clean benchmark.

  • Narrow source coverage. The corpus focused on company research, not macro, sector, fund, or fixed-income analysis.
  • Aggressive source caps. The balancing rule discarded 2,320 rows that had already passed every quality gate.
  • A contaminated local split. We reserved 73 rows, then later trained on the combined file. That split cannot support a clean held-out claim.
  • No final human review. Automated checks can verify structure, provenance, and leakage, but not economic judgment.
  • Too many variables changed. The task, corpus, model, and training recipe moved together, so the gain cannot be attributed to one component.

Attempt 1 established the extraction and training path, but its narrow corpus and contaminated local split made scale the next question.

Attempt #2: Scaling 50x

Data

Attempt 2 began after an office-hours session with Sara Hooker, co-founder of Adaption Labs. Sara emphasized the importance of training with substantially more data. That advice changed our next question: instead of continuing to optimize a small proof of concept, we expanded the source corpus and tested whether the full workflow could hold up at scale.

Attempt 2 addressed the narrow source coverage. We manually expanded the corpus from roughly 1,500 to about 7,000 reports, adding macro outlooks, sector studies, fund reviews, and fixed-income commentary from institutional sources.

The broader corpus required a broader task model. Each row records a task family, an asset family, and the relationship between its evidence and conclusion. This taxonomy also adds a task that Attempt 1 lacked: deciding when the evidence is insufficient.

More than one third of the rows reward the model for declining to make an unsupported claim. That task matters because fluent synthesis is not enough; an analyst also needs to recognize when the available evidence cannot support a conclusion.

We ran Adaptive Data three times, using three recipes, and merged the outputs with Combine. The three generations used the same source corpus but expressed each analytical task differently. The combined dataset therefore increased row diversity without pretending that each generated row came from an independent source.

The larger corpus entered Adaptive Data at a higher baseline quality. Each source generation started at 7.0, while the combined dataset started at 8.0 and finished at 9.4. The three generations share the same 1,753 leakage groups, so they represent roughly 15,250 unique generated items rather than three independent corpora. The headline scale came from combining broader source coverage with multiple adaptation recipes.

Model

AutoScientist trained a LoRA adapter for Llama 4 Scout 17B on the 45,758-row combined dataset. The released adapter records rank 32, alpha 64, dropout 0.05, and the q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, and o_proj targets. Trainer state records two epochs, 168 steps, and a peak learning rate of 2e-5.

Results

The adapted model won 80 to 20 in the dataset-specific preference evaluation. This makes Attempt 2 the stronger scaling result: the workflow absorbed broader source coverage, reached 45,758 rows, improved data quality from B to A, and retained a large preference margin on its own distribution.

Attempt 1 still provides the stronger evidence of external transfer because it includes a 77 to 23 Market Analysis category result. Attempt 2 has no equivalent category result, so we do not claim better generalization. The two attempts answer different questions: Attempt 1 proves transfer beyond its own rows, while Attempt 2 proves that the full data and training workflow can scale across a materially broader finance corpus.

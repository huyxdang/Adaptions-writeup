Equity reports contain the answers we wanted a model to produce, but not the questions needed to train it. We had to reconstruct that missing half of the conversation without letting the answer leak back into the prompt.

We tested the method twice. Attempt 1 turned 589 extracted examples into 27,862 adapted rows and produced a model preferred 77 to 23 on Adaption's broader Market Analysis evaluation. Attempt 2 widened the source corpus and produced 45,758 rows across three generations. This report shows the pipeline, the evidence, and the limits.

Reading the numbers. Every score reported here is a pairwise preference: the share of comparisons in which a judge chose the adapted model’s answer over the base model’s. A score of 77 therefore does not mean 77% correct.

Attempt 1: Prove the method

The source: answers without prompts

We started with roughly 1,500 sell-side reports on Vietnamese listed companies, published between 2011 and 2026. The collection included earnings reviews, valuation updates, and target-price revisions written by professional analysts. It did not include model-written text, blogs, or forum posts.

These reports contained the language we wanted the model to learn, but not the training structure we needed. An analyst writes a conclusion and the evidence behind it; the report does not record the instruction that produced that conclusion. Supervised fine-tuning requires both the instruction and the answer.

Extract: reconstruct the question

Our first five extraction approaches failed for the same reason: they generated plausible tasks that the source report did not actually answer. The version we kept works in reverse. It finds a real analyst conclusion first, removes that conclusion from the available evidence, and then reconstructs only the question that the evidence can support.

A fixed template exposed the problem clearly. It required counterevidence and an invalidation condition even when the source conclusion contained neither:

The v1 template demanded counterevidence and an invalidation condition on every row; 45 of 50 accepted completions supplied neither.

In a 50-row review, 45 accepted rows asked for unsupported content. Deriving the task from each conclusion removed that mismatch. The remaining quality gates then reduced the data to a smaller, traceable baseline.

Adaptive Data: expand the seed

We uploaded the 589 training rows to Adaption. Its Adaptive Data pipeline expanded them to 27,862 rows, of which 27,405 were used for training. The platform's quality score increased from 5.0 to 9.4, moving the dataset from grade C to grade A.

Adaptive Data changed the response structure more than the task. The prompt stayed close to the extracted evidence, while the completion became easier to inspect and evaluate.

AutoScientist: train and compare

AutoScientist trained a LoRA adapter on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct. The run used rank 16, alpha 32, all linear layers, two epochs at 1e-4, 382 steps, and loss on completions only. It produced two preference evaluations.

The second result is the stronger one. We constructed the first evaluation distribution; Adaption constructed the broader category evaluation. Winning 77 to 23 on that category is evidence that the behavior transferred beyond our own rows.

Limitations: what this run could not prove

Attempt 1 established a useful signal, but it did not establish a clean benchmark.

  • Narrow source coverage. The corpus focused on company research, not macro, sector, fund, or fixed-income analysis.
  • Aggressive source caps. The balancing rule discarded 2,320 rows that had already passed every quality gate.
  • A contaminated local split. We reserved 73 rows, then later trained on the combined file. That split cannot support a clean held-out claim.
  • No final human review. Automated checks can verify structure, provenance, and leakage, but not economic judgment.
  • Too many variables changed. The task, corpus, model, and training recipe moved together, so the gain cannot be attributed to one component.

Attempt 2: Scale the pipeline

More data: widen the source corpus

Attempt 2 addressed the narrow source coverage. We manually expanded the corpus from roughly 1,500 to about 7,000 reports, adding macro outlooks, sector studies, fund reviews, and fixed-income commentary from institutional sources.

The broader corpus required a broader task model. Each row records a task family, an asset family, and the relationship between its evidence and conclusion. This taxonomy also adds a task that Attempt 1 lacked: deciding when the evidence is insufficient.

More than one third of the rows reward the model for declining to make an unsupported claim. That task matters because fluent synthesis is not enough; an analyst also needs to recognize when the available evidence cannot support a conclusion.

We ran Adaptive Data three times, using three recipes, and merged the outputs with Combine. The three generations used the same source corpus but expressed each analytical task differently. The combined dataset therefore increased row diversity without pretending that each generated row came from an independent source.

Results: more data, incomplete evidence

The larger corpus entered Adaptive Data at a higher baseline quality. Each source generation started at 7.0, while the combined dataset started at 8.0 and finished at 9.4. The three generations share the same 1,753 leakage groups, so they represent roughly 15,250 unique source items rather than three independent corpora. The row count grew 78 times; the unique source material grew about 26 times.

AutoScientist trained Llama 4 Scout 17B on the combined set. The adapted model won 80 to 20 on the dataset-specific preference evaluation. The exact training configuration was not retained in the current export.

This result is not directly equivalent to Attempt 1. Attempt 2 did not produce the broader Market Analysis category score, so it shows fit to its own distribution but not external transfer. Attempt 1 therefore remains the stronger reported model result.

The next experiment is straightforward: freeze a genuinely unseen evaluation set, train each generation separately, then train on the combination. That ablation would show whether Combine adds useful diversity or repeats the same signal three times.

The two attempts answer different questions. Attempt 1 shows that answer-first extraction can transfer beyond its own dataset. Attempt 2 shows that the same workflow can support a much broader source corpus and multiple data-generation recipes. It does not yet show that the larger model generalizes better.
